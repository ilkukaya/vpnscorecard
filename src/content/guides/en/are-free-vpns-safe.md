A VPN sees all of your internet traffic. That makes the question "who pays for it?" extremely important.

## How free VPNs make money

Servers and bandwidth are expensive. A free VPN typically covers its costs with one or more of these:

- **Advertising**, often with tracking.
- **Collecting and selling browsing data** to data brokers or advertisers.
- **Selling your bandwidth**, turning your device into an exit point for other people's traffic.
- **Upselling** you to a paid plan — the only model that doesn't depend on your data.

## The risks

Security researchers have repeatedly found free VPN apps that:

- logged browsing activity despite "no-logs" claims, and later leaked it;
- used weak or no encryption;
- contained intrusive trackers or even malware;
- leaked DNS requests or your real IP address.

The common thread is a business model that treats users as the product.

## When a free VPN is fine

Free plans from established companies that sell paid subscriptions are a different story. The paid users fund the service, and the free plan is a way to try it. In our [best free VPN list](/best/free/) we only include plans like these. Typical limits:

- fewer server locations;
- a monthly data cap on some plans;
- no streaming-optimised or P2P servers;
- lower priority at busy times.

**Proton VPN's free plan** stands out because it has no data cap and no ads, and its apps are open source and independently audited.

## Red flags when choosing any VPN

- No clear company name, address or jurisdiction.
- No independent audit of its no-logs policy.
- Excessive app permissions (contacts, SMS, location).
- "Lifetime" plans at impossibly low prices.
- Reviews full of generic five-star praise.
